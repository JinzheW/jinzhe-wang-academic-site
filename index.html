Designing Expertise under Retention Constraints
Abstract
This paper studies the joint design of diagnostic expertise and compensation. A firm chooses a reusable information structure and performance pay before the worker decides whether to stay. Expertise improves diagnosis and shapes hidden implementation effort, but also raises the worker’s outside option. Different forms of expertise can have the same average diagnostic accuracy and outside value but different internal value. Optimal design concentrates precision on common problems and camouflages rarer ones within nested categories. As outside returns rise, the firm initially preserves expertise and raises the bonus. Once retention becomes sufficiently costly, it coarsens expertise and reduces the bonus, generating a nonmonotone compensation response. Retention pressure eliminates incremental stand-alone diagnostic value, not expertise in the limit: the diagnostic system still improves production. The model provides an informational theory of endogenous specificity.

Preference Aggregation under Social Pressure with Weicheng Min.
Abstract
This paper studies preference aggregation in sequential majority voting with two candidates. Voters have heterogeneous preferences, are uncertain about the distribution of preferences, and incur a social-pressure cost from voting for the losing candidate. We characterize all symmetric Markov equilibria exhibiting a two-stage structure: a learning stage followed by herding once either candidate’s lead reaches a threshold. We characterize the conditions for equilibria existence and show that stronger social pressure and greater distributional uncertainty hinder preference aggregation. We also identify a class of resistant equilibria, in which voters become more willing to act sincerely as their preferred candidate falls behind, until herding begins. Finally, we study welfare across voting positions. An influence–risk trade-off implies that early voters bear more risk but exert greater influence, while later voters are more protected but less influential. Consequently, although stronger social pressure reduces welfare for all voters, it makes later voters relatively better off than early voters within an equilibrium. Moreover, when comparing coexisting equilibria under the same parameters, improvements in preference aggregation need not be Pareto improving.
